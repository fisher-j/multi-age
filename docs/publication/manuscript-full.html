Regeneration and fuel loading with varying overstory retention in redwood stands

Working publication manuscript - accepted regeneration display; fuel Results for review

Author

Judson Fisher

Published

November 6, 2026

Abstract

Forest managers using retention forestry need information on regeneration and fuel development to design productive stands in fire-adapted ecosystems. We analyzed 10-year remeasurement data from the Redwood Multiaged Experiment in Jackson Demonstration State Forest, California. The experiment compared group selection, aggregated retention, and low- and high-density dispersed retention treatments and measured regeneration, sprout growth, and surface fuel loading.

Redwood regeneration responded strongly to increasing canopy openness, whereas tanoak responses were more modest. Sprout-height findings will remain part of the main regeneration evidence. Fuel measurements describe conditions before and after precommercial thinning, including the PCT-associated contrast. The manuscript will evaluate how overstory retention affects regeneration while presenting fuel components in a form useful for integrated silviculture, fuels, and fire management.

Introduction

Developing and maintaining multiaged stand structures requires silvicultural systems that create and sustain cohorts of different ages within the same stand (O’Hara, 1998; Schütz, 2002). Retention systems can retain a range of tree densities in dispersed or aggregated patterns and can be used to maintain multiaged stands or transform formerly even-aged stands (Mitchell & Beese, 2002).

Coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) is well suited to this objective because it regenerates reliably by stump sprouting and can grow beneath a retained overstory. Its response to light is nevertheless important: reduced access to light can limit sprout vigor and persistence (Berrill et al., 2018; Muma et al., 2022; O’Hara et al., 2007). Tanoak (Notholithocarpus densiflorus) is also shade tolerant and a vigorous resprouter, making its response relevant to both stand development and management of competing vegetation (Berrill et al., 2021; O’Hara et al., 2017).

Silvicultural decisions that promote regeneration also influence the fuel conditions in which later management and fire occur. This paper therefore examines regeneration under varying overstory retention and describes surface fuel conditions before and after precommercial thinning as reference information for integrated silvicultural, fuels, fire, and potential modeling applications.

We use 10-year measurements from the Redwood Multiaged Experiment to evaluate regeneration responses among retention treatments and to report fuel conditions before and after precommercial thinning, including their PCT-associated contrast. Redwood and tanoak provide the primary regeneration focus, with other species retained as descriptive context where useful. Fuel components will be presented in a publication-specific form after their analytical specification is reviewed.

Methods

Study area and design

The experiment is located in the Jackson Demonstration State Forest in coastal northern California. Four harvest treatments were replicated at four sites in 2012: group selection (GS), high-density dispersed retention (HD), high-density aggregated retention (HA), and low-density dispersed retention (LD). The retained-overstory treatments targeted post-harvest relative densities of 13% or 21%; group selection created a 1-ha opening with a lightly thinned buffer. These treatments were designed to examine alternative pathways for developing multiaged redwood stands (Berrill & O’Hara, 2009; Reineke, 1933).

Ten years after harvest, regeneration plots and fuel transects were established within each 0.2-ha treatment macro plot. Regeneration measurements characterized redwood, tanoak, and Douglas-fir; fuel transects characterized dead surface fuels and live vegetation. The plots were remeasured after precommercial thinning, which released selected conifers from competing trees and shrubs.

Analysis

Detailed sampling procedures, model diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses will be retained as supplementary material.

Regeneration basal area

Basal area was modeled separately for redwood, tanoak, Douglas-fir, and other species using zero-inflated Gamma mixed models with a conditional log link. Treatment was a fixed effect; site and macro plot nested within site were random intercepts. Each species category had its own intercept-only zero and dispersion components. Each model used 64 vegetation-plot observations from four sites and 16 macro plots. Arithmetic mean basal area included modeled zeros and integrated over both fitted Gaussian random-intercept distributions. Confidence intervals for the means were calculated using the log-scale delta method with the full parameter covariance, including random-effect variance uncertainty. Redwood and tanoak estimates are displayed in the Results; estimates for all four categories are reported in the supplement.

Fuel loads and PCT contrast

Reported values are model-based arithmetic mean loads, including modeled zeros and integrating over fitted random effects, with equal weights for the four study-site contexts.

These estimates provide model-based reference means for comparable forest conditions; 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the delta method with the full parameter covariance, including uncertainty in random-effect variances. Mean woody standing-vegetation reduction was estimated as the pre-PCT minus post-PCT contrast from the matched repeated-phase model, with its joint covariance, and interpreted as a potential surface-fuel contribution.

Fuel conditions before and after precommercial thinning

Dead fuel loading

Fuel measurements ten years after harvest characterized conditions before and after precommercial thinning (PCT) across the four retention treatments (Figure 2). Following PCT, group selection (GS) and low-density dispersed retention (LD) showed the largest estimated increases in fine dead-fuel loads and reductions in woody standing vegetation. These treatments also had the highest post-PCT fine dead-fuel loads. Pre-PCT dead-fuel estimates suggested subtler, component-specific treatment patterns. Fuel loads are reported as model-estimated means, with 95% confidence intervals. Woody and herbaceous standing vegetation are reported separately, as are pre-PCT litter and duff. All loads and load differences below are in Mg ha-1.

Before PCT, estimated 1-hour fuel loads were highest in high-density aggregated retention (HA; 1.2) and lowest in GS (0.6), with LD and high-density dispersed (HD) retention intermediate. Estimated 10-hour loads ranged from 2.8 in HD to 3.8 in GS, and 100-hour loads from 9.0 to 11.5 in those same treatments. The 1,000-hour class had larger mean loads, ranging from 30.2 in LD to 49.6 (95% CI 25.9 to 95.0) in GS. Uncertainty was substantial for this coarse-wood component across treatments.

The separated forest-floor components showed different treatment patterns. Pre-PCT litter estimates ranged from 12.6 in LD to 18.4 in GS; HA and HD were intermediate. Duff estimates were lowest in HA, at 19.3 (95% CI 14.5 to 25.8), and highest in HD, at 35.5 (95% CI 26.6 to 47.4), with GS and LD both near 28–29. These are model-derived forest-floor loads based on the estimated litter-depth boundary. Post-PCT litter and duff were not estimated because the forest-floor boundary could not be distinguished reliably in the presence of slash and suspended foliage.

After PCT, estimated 1-hour loads were about 2.4–2.7 in GS, LD, and HD, and 1.4 in HA. The 10-hour estimates were ordered GS (9.6), LD (5.9), HA (4.1), and HD (3.3). The 100-hour estimates followed the same order, from 19.6 (95% CI 11.8 to 32.8) in GS to 7.5 (95% CI 4.6 to 12.2) in HD. Post-PCT 1,000-hour means ranged from 25.2 in HA to 53.9 in GS, with wide confidence intervals. These rankings describe estimated loads; they do not establish pairwise treatment differences. The separate dead-fuel phase estimates are reference conditions, without paired phase-change tests in this display.

Standing vegetation and woody vegetation difference

Pre-PCT woody standing-vegetation means were greatest in GS (39.9), followed by LD (26.5), HD (18.8), and HA (14.7). Post-PCT means were 2.0–2.9 across treatments. The joint phase model estimated pre-minus-post reductions of 37.4 (95% CI 13.4 to 61.4) in GS, 24.5 (95% CI 9.0 to 40.0) in LD, 11.8 (95% CI 3.6 to 20.0) in HA, and 16.6 (95% CI 5.5 to 27.6) in HD. All four reduction intervals excluded zero. Their uncertainty includes the pre/post covariance; the ordering of reduction estimates alone does not test differences among treatments.

Herbaceous standing-vegetation means were small relative to woody loads: 0.24–0.59 before PCT and 0.11–0.35 afterward, with broad relative uncertainty. The woody reduction represents an inferred potential contribution to surface fuels. It does not measure slash deposition or reconcile standing-vegetation loss with changes in downed fuel classes. Without a contemporaneous unthinned comparison, the phase transition does not isolate a causal PCT effect.

Discussion
